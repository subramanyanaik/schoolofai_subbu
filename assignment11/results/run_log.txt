   it is also the one it causes -- which is why eps is a hyperparameter and not a constant.

the two decay rules differ by 3.239e-05 after five steps at lambda=0.1, and both
reproduce torch to 16 digits -- so the difference is the algorithm, not the arithmetic.

  mechanisms aimed at the same hundred steps.

This is the opposite of what the schedule suggests.  Warmup's per-step contribution is
log((t+1)/t), which is 0.69 at step 2 and falls like 1/t; Adam's is roughly flat.  So:

  warmup is the larger term for steps 2 to 26
  from step 27 to the end of warmup at 100, Adam's own transient moves the ratio more
  after step 100 the learning rate is constant, d log lr = 0 exactly, and warmup contributes nothing

Over the whole warmup, the schedule accounts for 36% of the total movement in log ratio,
and it spends almost all of that in the first 26 steps.

So the answer has two halves and they are different numbers:
  warmup stops CHANGING the ratio at step 100 -- by construction, since eta stops changing;
  warmup stops being what DRIVES the ratio at step 26, which is measured, and which is
  3x earlier than the schedule would lead you to believe.

The reason SP misses is worth more than the check: at lr=0.002, width 1024 is far past

diagnostic is confounded by exactly the condition it is supposed to detect.

CONCLUSION, against the folklore: the update-to-weight ratio is a good instrument for
'is this layer moving at a sane speed', and a bad one for 'is this model parameterised
correctly at a new width'.  The quantity that answers the second question is the scale of
the activations, and section 5e measures it.

3. And the arm that did NOT work, because it is the one I expected to win: cosine re-planned

   that was 'interrupted' had simply taken bigger steps for longer.  At this horizon, being
   caught mid-decay beats having decayed.

And the question everyone actually wants answered -- does WSD beat cosine?  At its own
300-step horizon WSD reaches 2.0478 against cosine's 2.1050: -0.0572 nats,
2.0x the noise floor.  On this run, WSD wins, and the margin is resolvable.

What that does NOT license is the general claim.  This is 1.6M parameters, 300 steps,
three seeds, one depth, one batch size, and a stable phase 220 steps long.  The published WSD results
are about runs where that phase is thousands of times longer, and the mechanism usually
offered for why it works -- that a long high-LR phase finds wider basins -- cannot be
tested at this scale at all.  A result that agrees with the literature for reasons this
experiment cannot check is still only one data point.

What this run does establish is narrower and more useful: WSD's cooldown can be aimed at
a stopping point chosen after the run started, and doing so is worth +0.0385 nats against
a cosine schedule that had to commit to its horizon in advance.

    against the best of them.  4096 is one further doubling, 16x the base width.

    guarantees are about width, and depth transfer is a separate and weaker claim.
    The horizon is 200 steps, the batch is 16x128 tokens, and the optimal
    learning rate depends on both -- neither was swept, so this number is the best
    learning rate *for this recipe*, not a constant of the architecture.
  The width that was not run. 4096 does not fit in 4.0 GiB at 16 bytes
    per parameter, so the last doubling is an extrapolation in every case.

  So: HIGH confidence that 2.9e-03 is within a factor of 2 of the optimum at 4096 for
  this recipe -- a factor of 2 being the resolution the sweep itself has.  The transfer
  was given a chance to fail at a width it had never seen, and did not.

  Either way: no confidence in a third significant figure, and no claim at all about a
  different depth, batch size or token budget.  The practical form of that is that I would
  run one three-point confirmation sweep at the target width before committing a long run --
