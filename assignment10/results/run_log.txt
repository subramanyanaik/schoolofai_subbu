What the distance to 40% is made of   [t+6m46s]
============================================================================================
  best measured MFU                        31.4%   + batch 32
  ceiling from the matmul shapes alone     60.0%   nothing but the matmuls, at their measured rates

  100% -> 60.0%  (40.0 points): shape. Gone before the loop starts.
  60.0% -> 31.4%  (28.6 points): everything that is not a matmul.

  So 40% is *not* out of reach at this model shape -- the shapes allow 60.0%. The
  loop captures 52% of what they allow; to hit 40% it would have to capture 67%.
  On this machine the distance to 40% is a loop-and-batch-size problem, not a
  model-shape problem, and that is the opposite of what I expected to find.

  The 40.0 points of shape, itemised: the output head is 80% of the arithmetic
  and is one [B*T,256]x[256,50257] matmul. With K=256 a tensor core spends most of
  its time reading rather than multiplying, and it reaches 61% of the
  square-matmul rate. The worst-shaped one is attn c_proj at 42%, but it is only
  1.6% of the work. A wider model fixes this; a better loop does not.

  The 28.6 points the loop loses, itemised as far as I measured them:
    - the optimizer and gradient clipping: 14% of the step at batch 8, pure memory
      traffic over 16M parameters x 4 tensors, contributing nothing to the numerator;
    - LayerNorm, GELU, softmax and the residual adds, all memory-bound;
    - fixed per-step launch and Python overhead, which is why the sweep runs 5.9% at
      batch 1 and 31.4% at batch 32; a 4 GiB card is why it stops there;
    - no kernel fusion: torch.compile does not run in this environment
      (backend='inductor' raised:...), so every elementwise op is
      its own launch.

  Not claimed: that those bullets sum to 28.6 points. They are measured
  contributions, not a partition, and some of the gap is unaccounted for. The
  largest lever I actually had was precision and batch size together, worth
  18.0 points; the largest one I did not have is making the model wider.

  One caveat over all of it: the worst spread between repeat timings of the *same*
  matmul on this laptop was 32.8%. These percentages are honest to about their first
  digit, and the ordering between them, not to their third.

============================================================================================
Which one I would train in   [t+7m16s]
============================================================================================
bf16 for the compute; fp32 for the master weights, the optimizer state and the loss.

The case, in the order the measurements make it:
  1. Range is what gradients need, and range is what bf16 keeps. bf16 has fp32's
     own 8 exponent bits, so 0.00% of this step's 16,058,112 gradient
     entries fall below its smallest normal. fp16 has 5 exponent bits, and
     81.7% of them fall below *its* smallest normal.
  2. Resolution is what bf16 gives up, and it turns out not to matter. Casting the
     real gradient to bf16 costs 1.7e-03 relative L2 error, moves 0.00% of entries
     by more than 10%, and flushes 0.00% to zero.
  3. fp16's better L2 error is a trap. It reads 2.1e-04 -- better than bf16 -- while
     sending 18.60% of the entries to exactly zero, because L2 error is set by the
     largest entries and the smallest ones are where the rare-token updates live.
     Recovering those needs loss scaling: machinery, a scale factor to tune, and a
     failure mode (overflow, skipped steps) that bf16 simply does not have.
  4. fp8 E4M3 is not a training format for gradients at this scale. Unscaled it
     destroys 97.2% of them; with one scale factor per tensor it still destroys
     68.1%. Its 3 mantissa bits also put 1.6% of error on a number as
     ordinary as 0.1. It can work -- but with finer-grained scaling than one factor
     per tensor, and on hardware that has fp8 tensor cores. This card is sm_86,
     which does not, so every fp8 number above is a software cast: the numerical
     cost with none of the speed that would justify paying it.
  5. And it is free. Measured above: 1.2x faster for -0.0015 nats over 300 steps.

What stays in fp32, and why the answer is not 'bf16 everywhere': the master weights
and Adam's moments. A weight update is a small number added to a large one, and
bf16's 8 mantissa bits resolve about 0.0039 relative -- an update smaller than
that vanishes entirely when the sum is rounded back. So: bf16 activations and
matmuls, fp32 accumulation inside them, fp32 master weights, fp32 optimizer state.
That is the 16 bytes per parameter counted in section 1,
and it is what the extra bytes are buying.
