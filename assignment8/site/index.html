There are exactly two bills

Everything on this page is an attempt to pay less of one or both. They pull against each other: the tricks that shrink the cache usually cost quality, and the tricks that keep quality do not shrink the cache. Knowing which bill a mechanism attacks tells you almost everything about when to use it.

Bill 1 — compute

The N×N score matrix

Every query scores every key. 6 tokens is 36 numbers, which sounds free. 1,000 tokens is a million. 1 million tokens is a trillion — per head, per layer. Doubling the context quadruples the work. This is what makes training long-context models expensive.

Bill 2 — memory

The KV cache that never shrinks

At generation time you cannot recompute K and V for the whole history at every step, so you store them. Every token you emit permanently adds bytes, per head, per layer, per user. This one grows only linearly — and it is still the bill that decides how many people you can serve.

Start here: the plain scaled dot product

Nothing after this makes sense without it. This is running the real arithmetic on a toy sentence — six tokens, four dimensions — not a picture of it. Change the controls and watch which of the properties downstream mechanisms will spend a decade trying to buy back.

1. project

Each token becomes a Query (what am I looking for), a Key (what do I offer) and a Value (what I actually pass on).

2. score

QKᵀ — one matmul, every pair at once. This is the matrix that costs N².

3. scale

Divide by √dk. Dot products of random d-dim vectors have variance d, so without this the logits are far too wide and softmax saturates into a one-hot. Gradients die.

4. mask

Set the future to −∞, because e−∞ = 0. Purely to make a parallel matmul behave causally.

5. softmax

Amplifies by absolute gap, not relative gap — try the amplitude slider. It also forces the row to sum to 1, which creates competition, and forces every weight positive.

6. read out

Weighted sum of the Values. Note step 5 is the only row-wise nonlinearity — and it is the single thing preventing you from reassociating to KᵀV first. Remove it and you get linear attention.

Position lab: how a token learns where it is

The whole length story lives here. Four ideas, in order of appearance, and then the three ways people tried to stretch RoPE past the length it was trained on.

RoPE: why the gap is what survives

Two tokens on the unit circle, rotated by their positions. Drag both. The absolute angles change; the angle between them does not — and the dot product only sees that difference. That is the whole trick, and it is an algebraic identity, not a learned tendency.

Score vs distance: RoPE, ALiBi, sliding window

How much attention a token can still get, as a function of how far away it is. ALiBi's extrapolation works by making distant tokens exponentially unlikely — which is also exactly why it does not help long-range retrieval.

Stretching RoPE past its training length

Each RoPE dimension pair has its own wavelength. Dimensions whose wavelength is shorter than the training context have been seen rotating many times — they are fine. Dimensions whose wavelength is longer have never completed a rotation, and those are the ones that go out of distribution when you extend. The three methods differ entirely in which dimensions they squash.

KV cache calculator

This is the bill that decides how many users you can serve. Set your architecture and watch the KV-shape mechanisms fight over it. Note that GQA, MQA and MLA all divide the constant — only the hybrid schedule and the linear layers change how it scales.

cache = 2 (K and V) × layers × kv_heads × head_dim × bytes × tokens × users. MLA replaces kv_heads×head_dim with a single learned latent (here dkv=512 plus a 64-dim decoupled RoPE part, DeepSeek-V2's configuration). The hybrid row assumes 1 full-attention layer in 4.

What happens when you delete softmax

Softmax is a row-wise nonlinearity, so it must see the whole row before it can normalise — that is what forces you to build the N×N matrix. Take it out and the parentheses move: compute KᵀV first and the entire past collapses into one d×d matrix that never grows. Then spend four years buying back what you lost.

The state does not grow

Add, or overwrite? — the delta rule

Memory currently holds 40 for this key. The model now wants it to hold 55. Plain linear attention only knows how to add.

The 2026 answer: don't choose, interleave

A fixed-size state provably cannot hold N ≫ d exact facts. That is an information-theoretic bound, not an engineering problem — which is why every shipping model that uses linear layers also keeps a minority of full-attention layers. Drag the ratio and watch what it does to the cache.
